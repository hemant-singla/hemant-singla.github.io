Johnson & Johnson · Healthcare procurement

Supplier rationalization: fewer suppliers, stronger agreements.

J&J has gone from a reported 80,000 suppliers to about 39,000. How much of that is real rationalization, could a rule-based model have made those calls, and how far could it go next?

Independent study. J&J does not publish supplier-level data, so every method runs on 344,504 real public purchase orders (State of California, FY2012–15) and the result is scaled to J&J’s published totals. Not J&J work: nothing was implemented, and the J&J figures are modeled, not realized savings.

Key results

  • 30–42%J&J’s own reduction to ~39,000 once the Kenvue spin-off is taken out; our rules produce a cut of that size
  • ~32,100suppliers after the Phase 2 category model, from ~39,000 today, without adding dominant-supplier categories
  • $134Ma year measured in the data at J&J scale ($414M in the base case, which assumes a 0.89% re-tender saving)

Kenvue publishes no supplier count, so its share is estimated: 15.7% if in line with its share of J&J’s 2022 sales, 30% as a high case. That leaves 56,000–67,400 J&J suppliers, and a reduction of 30–42% to reach ~39,000. Applied to that base, this project’s rules give 31,200–49,000: J&J’s own reduction is the size the method produces. That does not show J&J used these rules, only that a cut of that size can be made with a reason behind every decision.

How much rests on the assumption

$280M of the $414M base case is the re-tender saving on kept Leverage suppliers, which the data cannot measure. Each 0.1 point of re-tender saving adds about $19M a year, so the claim becomes testable: the $300M goal needs at least 0.89% (0.26–1.49% across the range of measured savings). A first sourcing wave under the MSA’s benchmark clause shows whether that holds before the rest is committed.

Phase 2: going further from ~39,000

After the blanket rules, most remaining suppliers are legitimate, so Phase 2 uses a different model: a target count per category. Keep the fewest suppliers that cover 90% of the category’s spend, with at least two in Strategic and Bottleneck categories; always keep strategic, sole-source and small or diverse suppliers. Everyone else moves volume to the category’s core suppliers or the catalog.

Ten categories newly cross 50%, worth $64M over three years (0.3% of active spend); they go on a watch-list and get a second-source clause. Every sole-source supplier is kept: the first version of the rules let one sole supplier be consolidated, a test caught it, and rule R2 was fixed.

Checking the duplicate matching

70 accepted pairs were checked by hand, using the names, ZIP code, main category and department. Exact name matches were right 20 of 20 times; fuzzy matches (similarity 0.88–0.99) 39 of 50 times. That is about 96% precision overall but 78% in the fuzzy band, with errors like “United Rentals” vs “United Van Rentals”. So exact matches merge automatically and fuzzy matches go to a review queue first. They touch 0.6% of spend.

Recommendation

Phase 1, the tail first: merge duplicate records and move one-time vendors to catalogs (low risk, pays back on process savings alone). Then consolidate within Leverage and Non-critical categories, where the price gap exists, and re-tender them under volume-tiered MSAs. Leave Strategic and Bottleneck categories out of consolidation. Phase 2: set a target count per category (90% spend coverage, at least two suppliers where supply is risky). Never cut a strategic or sole-source supplier; put them on partnership MOUs and add second sources for bottleneck items. Approve the re-tender only once the first MSAs show the 0.89% saving the base case needs.

The finding that changed the plan

My first top-down estimate was $385M a year. The price-gap test on 127 goods items bought from both kept and exiting suppliers showed exiting suppliers charged 9.8% more in Leverage and 5.2% more in Non-critical categories, but were cheaper in Bottleneck (−10%) and Strategic (−7%) ones. So price savings only exist where the market is competitive, only $134M of the base case is measured, and the plan pushes price only in Leverage and Non-critical categories.

Real J&J suppliers

Read from the suppliers’ own SEC 10-K filings. Alkermes is the exclusive maker of RISPERDAL CONSTA; MeiraGTx manufactures J&J’s RPGR gene therapy under a 4+1-year supply agreement; Mersana’s clinical supply deal gives J&J a technology-transfer right. Emergent is the counter-example: J&J ended its COVID-19 vaccine contract in 2022 over manufacturing problems, which is why the rules protect sole-source and bottleneck suppliers.

Method

344,504 raw purchase-order lines cleaned in Python and SQLite, every raw row kept with its outcome and reason code. Spend cube and Pareto / ABC segmentation in SQL window functions; a Kraljic matrix of 409 categories; duplicate suppliers found with TF-IDF character n-grams and cosine similarity (scikit-learn); a weighted scorecard and rules R1–R8 for the keep / consolidate / exit decision; a measured price-gap test and low / base / high savings scenarios; a back-test against J&J’s reported counts with the Kenvue spin-off taken out; and a Phase 2 category target model. Results go to an Excel workbook with live formulas, a Power BI dashboard, a business case and agreement templates (MOU, MSA, supply agreement, catalog terms, transition notices). Twenty-one tests check that strategic and sole-source suppliers are never exited, that every supplier and raw row has a reason code, and that totals reconcile from the raw file to the decisions.

Limitations

Public-sector purchasing is a proxy for J&J’s spend, so category mix and prices differ; J&J figures are scaled, not measured. $280M of the $414M base case is an assumption about re-tender savings. J&J’s 80,000 figure is undated, Kenvue’s supplier share is estimated, and the FY2025 count uses a new counting method. Supplier quality, risk scores and contract terms were not available; the price gap uses goods only. Small/diverse status comes from California certifications. Duplicate precision rests on a 70-pair hand check.
