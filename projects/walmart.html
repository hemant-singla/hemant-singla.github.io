Walmart · Consumer goods retail

Forecast reliability and review priorities.

Which forecasts are reliable, and which five products deserve a planner’s attention each week?

Independent study on public M5 data (Walmart sales, 2011–2016). Not Walmart work; nothing was deployed and no savings are claimed.
Why I picked this problem. At Samsung, pre-release triage meant deciding which of many issues deserved attention before a fixed launch date. Demand planners face the same question every week with forecasts: which few items deserve their limited review time? This study tests that with real sales data.
View code on GitHub Read the decision memo

Key results

  • 21%lower forecast error than the seasonal-naive baseline on the final 28-day holdout (RMSSE 0.733 vs 0.932)
  • 89%of holdout actuals fell inside the calibrated 90% forecast ranges
  • 8%daily forecast error across all 200 items combined on the final 28-day holdout (WAPE 7.9%)

How accurate, at the level planners work at

The 57.9% WAPE in the table below is scored on every product, in every store, on every day. Most of these items sell a few units a day and often none, so missing by one or two units is a large share of the sale. That is normal for slow sellers, but planners rarely act at that level. Summing the same forecasts before scoring tells a different story: across all 200 items combined, the daily forecast was off by about 8%.

Recommendation

Use the global LightGBM forecast with ETS as a fallback. Pick the weekly review list with a simple rule: largest recent error, or top sellers. The tuned combined score I designed did not show a real advantage over those rules. Add a second short list for forecasts that are unusually bad for that item, plus a flag for active items with long zero-sales runs.

LightGBM ranked first in all six validation periods and in every holdout segment, but only by 0.002–0.025 RMSSE over ETS, which runs about 500× faster. On low-volume items the two are tied.

What the bias means for stock

Every model under-forecast the holdout: LightGBM by 4.9% (about 740 units short of the 15,005 sold), ETS by 6.8% and seasonal naive by 10.6%. Ordering to these forecasts as they stand would leave shelves short, so stockout risk rises unless safety stock covers the gap. The shortfall is not spread evenly. LightGBM was 8.5% under on weekends and 2.8% under on weekdays, so the busiest days are where shelves would run out first.

In practice that means sizing safety stock from the upper end of the calibrated 90% ranges rather than the point forecast, and watching whether the weekend gap persists before correcting for it. This data has no inventory or lead-time figures, so the stockout and service-level effect can’t be measured here; that is the question my planned inventory study takes up.

Share of next-week absolute forecast error captured. The honest finding: simple rules work as well as the score I tuned, and all of them catch about 5× more error than random. Random is a low bar, so the useful comparison is between the sensible rules. Gaps between sensible rules are about half a percentage point and change week to week. Lists ranked by units mostly pick big sellers; a list ranked by item-normalised error catches unusually bad forecasts (60% precision) but only ~4% of unit error, so the two goals need two lists.

Method

200 FOODS/HOUSEHOLD product-store series from stores CA_1 and TX_1 (388,200 daily records, reconciled in DuckDB with 26/26 audit checks). Seasonal naive, ETS and LightGBM were compared in a 60-origin weekly rolling backtest; every forecast uses only data up to its forecast date, enforced by tests. The final 28 days were sealed in code until all model and rule choices were frozen, then used once. Results were independently recomputed in SQL (0 mismatches) and presented in a 10-page Power BI dashboard.

Failure case

The largest holdout miss, FOODS_3_444_CA_1, had no recorded sales for 333 days while still priced, then sold 414 units against a forecast of 3. Every ranking rule placed it near the bottom, because error-based signals cannot see an event with no recent history. All models also under-forecast the holdout, LightGBM by 15% on low-volume items.

Limitations

No inventory, lead-time, margin or planner-override data, so stockouts, service level and cash impact cannot be measured. Recorded sales may understate demand. Two stores, two categories, no sparse or new items; the holdout is 28 days. Panel RMSSE is not the official M5 WRMSSE.
